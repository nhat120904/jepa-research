\begin{abstract}
Latent world-model planners often select actions by minimizing the distance
between a predicted outcome and a goal in representation space. We ask whether
this terminal cost can fail even when every candidate outcome is exact. On
MetaWorld contact tasks, we replace learned rollouts with simulator execution
while holding the encoder and CEM budget fixed. Latent $L_2$ solves $0/64$ push
and pick-place episodes using the released DINOv2-based representation, while a
physical reference cost solves $64/64$ push and $49/64$ pick-place episodes.
The failure is task-dependent: latent $L_2$ also fails button-press and
window-close but solves drawer-close. On the candidate populations CEM creates,
latent-$L_2$/physical-cost agreement is weak or absent initially and becomes
negative after refitting. In matched CEM branches that differ only in refitting
cost, latent-$L_2$ refitting produces candidates $1.13$--$2.18$\,cm worse in
task distance. A supervised state readout provides a complementary diagnostic:
it has good broad held-out rank agreement but modest elite recovery, both of
which degrade further on CEM-generated candidates. A second encoder on
OGBench-Cube also exhibits residual misranking after predicted endpoints are
replaced by rendered outcomes. These results argue for evaluating planning
costs on the candidate distributions created by search, not only on held-out
trajectory metrics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Visual world models increasingly plan by predicting future representations and
minimizing their distance to a goal
\citep{zhou2024dinowm,jepawms2025,maes2026lewm}. This approach can fail because
the prediction is wrong, because search never proposes a useful action, or
because latent distance prefers the wrong outcome. These explanations are easy
to conflate: a failed episode alone does not reveal which component failed.

This paper isolates the third possibility. We ask a simple question: \emph{if
every candidate outcome is exact, does the latent cost choose the physically
better one?} In MetaWorld~\citep{yu2020metaworld}, we save the simulator state,
execute every candidate action sequence, render its endpoint, and encode that
image. The learned predictor is removed, while the representation, goal image,
terminal cost, and search budget stay unchanged. Simulator state supplies a
physical reference cost and verifies that the same search can solve the task.
We evaluate two released MetaWorld planning stacks, DINO-WM and JEPA-WM; both
route through the same frozen \texttt{dinov2\_vits14} encoder once their
predictors are bypassed, so the MetaWorld study probes one representation
through two released implementations, and a second, independently trained
encoder on OGBench-Cube tests whether the pattern is specific to that
representation.

The answer is often no. With exact candidate outcomes, latent $L_2$ fails all
64 push and pick-place episodes for both released planning stacks, whereas the
physical cost solves all push episodes and 49 pick-place episodes. Candidate-
level measurements explain the gap: the costs rank the same outcomes
differently, and repeated CEM updates amplify that disagreement. A second cost
based on decoded object and hand positions looks accurate on held-out expert
frames but also loses rank agreement on CEM's candidates. Directly changing
only the refitting cost produces worse later candidates. Residual true-endpoint
misranking also appears with a distinct encoder on
OGBench-Cube, although learned prediction error explains part of the total
regret there.

Our audit has three steps. First, exact simulator execution asks whether a
terminal cost fails without prediction error. Second, it evaluates the ordering
that cost induces on the candidates CEM actually creates. Third, matched CEM
branches change the refitting cost while holding the initial population,
snapshots, and base noise fixed. This identifies whether cost ordering changes
later proposals. Across MetaWorld and OGBench-Cube, the audit shows that
terminal-cost misranking can survive exact outcome prediction. Stateprobe
further shows why average held-out accuracy is not enough: broad rank agreement
need not imply reliable elite selection, especially after search shifts the
candidate distribution. The drawer-close boundary limits the conclusion to
tasks where latent geometry fails to preserve task-relevant ordering.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Latent planning and goal geometry.}
DINO-WM plans in the frozen feature space of a self-supervised visual encoder
\citep{zhou2024dinowm}; the released MetaWorld stacks evaluated here come from
a later study of joint-embedding world models~\citep{jepawms2025}.
LeWorldModel trains a compact encoder and world model end to end and provides
the OGBench-Cube checkpoint used in our second study
\citep{maes2026lewm,maes2026stablewm,park2025ogbench}. Other approaches give
latent geometry a behavioral target: contrastive goal-conditioned RL learns
similarities for goal reaching~\citep{eysenbach2022contrastive}, and temporal-
distance learning predicts how many steps separate two states
\citep{myers2024temporal}. Our question is diagnostic rather than
constructive: does an already trained geometry rank the candidates encountered
during planning in the right order?

\paragraph{Decision-aware models and objectives.}
Predictive accuracy need not measure usefulness inside a planner. Value-aware
model learning weights errors by their effect on value
\citep{farahmand2017vaml,farahmand2018itervaml}; value equivalence formalizes
when different models support the same planning computations
\citep{grimm2020ve}; and MuZero and control-oriented model learning train
models through decision-relevant targets
\citep{schrittwieser2020muzero,nikishin2022omd}. Recent latent-planning methods
similarly learn reachability, trajectory-ordering, or action-consistency
objectives~\citep{rcaux2026,trm2026,acid2026}, while latent-geometry methods can
amortize planning itself~\citep{nguyen2026latentgeometry}. We complement this
work by holding the learned representation fixed and measuring its terminal
cost against physical outcomes.

\paragraph{Optimization can expose model and score errors.}
Policies can exploit learned-dynamics errors~\citep{janner2019trust}, and model
objectives may disagree with control performance~\citep{lambert2020objective}.
Planning can also move action sequences away from the training distribution
\citep{parthasarathy2025traintest}. Even without such structure, selecting the
minimum of noisy estimates creates an optimizer's curse
\citep{smith2006optimizer}; under repeated optimization, a proxy can diverge
further from its target~\citep{gao2023overopt,skalse2022defining,
karwowski2024goodhart}. IMWM separately highlights limited proposal coverage
when environment rollout replaces learned dynamics~\citep{imwm2026}. Our
experiments score the same candidate outcomes with latent and physical costs,
then change only the score used to update CEM. Scoring identical candidate
outcomes separates ranking error from prediction error and candidate-set
contents; the matched-refit intervention then tests how that ranking affects
subsequent proposals, following the broader call to evaluate world models by
downstream decisions~\citep{yu2026wmeval}.

\subsection{Models, tasks, and planning protocol}

We evaluate the released \texttt{dino\_wm\_metaworld} and
\texttt{jepa\_wm\_metaworld} checkpoints through their public
\texttt{EncPredWM} interface~\citep{jepawms2025}. Both use their released
preprocessing and action normalization. Both stacks use the same frozen
\texttt{dinov2\_vits14} encoder; once their predictors are bypassed, they are
two released-stack checks of the same representation. The contact tasks are
\texttt{mw-push} and \texttt{mw-pick-place}; \texttt{mw-reach} is used only as
a free-space control. The goal image is the scripted expert trajectory's final
frame. Episode success is the simulator's task flag at the final environment
step, not an any-step latch. Its object-to-goal distance radii are $5$\,cm for
push and $7$\,cm for pick-place.

Unless otherwise stated, the candidate-level experiments use receding-horizon CEM
\citep{rubinstein1997cem} with 100 candidates, a top-10\% elite set, six
refitting iterations, and action horizon $H=6$. We denote this budget by
$100\times6$. The simulator-state cost is a positive control for the planner
and budget.

The OGBench study uses the independently trained end-to-end
\texttt{quentinll/lewm-cube} checkpoint and the official OGBench
\texttt{cube\_single\_expert} dataset~\citep{maes2026lewm,park2025ogbench}. Its
ViT-tiny encoder is distinct from the MetaWorld DINOv2 encoder. We evaluate one
checkpoint on one Cube task.

For task breadth, a pre-specified scripted-expert gate retained five additional
MetaWorld tasks; peg-insert-side failed this gate. We then ran paired
exact-dynamics reference and latent-$L_2$ arms. Because the released stacks
share an encoder, this breadth check uses DINO-WM only. A task is interpreted
only when the physical reference establishes finite-budget solvability; all
cells, including failed reference controls, are reported. The full eligibility
rule and seed ranges are in the supplement.

\subsection{Exact candidate execution and three terminal costs}

At each replan, the evaluation harness saves the full MuJoCo state. For
every action sequence $a$, it restores the same snapshot, executes $a$ in the
simulator, renders the terminal observation, and encodes that observation with
the frozen visual encoder. Thus candidate transitions are exact, while the
representation and terminal scorer remain unchanged.

We compare three costs:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Latent $L_2$}, the released Euclidean distance between the
  encoded endpoint and goal.
  \item \textbf{Stateprobe}, a supervised diagnostic that decodes
  object position $\hat o$ and end-effector position $\hat h$ with
  supervised probes on frozen
  endpoint tokens using simulator labels, then applies the planning cost
  \begin{equation}
    \hat c(a)=\|\hat o_T(a)-\hat o_g\|_2+
       0.5\|\hat h_T(a)-\hat o_T(a)\|_2.
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Simulator-state reference}, which applies the same form to
  simulator object and hand state:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:state-reference}
    c^*(a)=\|o_T(a)-o_g\|_2+
       0.5\|h_T(a)-o_T(a)\|_2.
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The reference is a shaped physical cost; we also report task success and direct
object-to-goal distance. Stateprobe combines the frozen representation with a
supervised readout and a fixed functional form, so results below diagnose
whether task-relevant state is \emph{accessible through this readout and
cost}, not whether the encoder discards it; Appendix~\ref{app:probe}
discusses this distinction further.

\subsection{Measuring cost quality during search}

For each CEM population, we compare the learned and physical costs by Spearman
rank agreement, reference top-10\% recall, and selection regret. Under the
shaped reference, this is
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{sel}} =
 c^*\!\left(\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}\hat c(a)\right)
 - \min_{a\in\mathcal A}c^*(a).
\end{equation}
For the two contact tasks, we additionally report direct physical task-distance
regret,
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{phys}} =
 d_{\mathrm{task}}\!\left(\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}\hat c(a)\right)
 - \min_{a\in\mathcal A}d_{\mathrm{task}}(a), \qquad
 d_{\mathrm{task}}(a)=\|o_T(a)-o_g\|_2.
\end{equation}
Supplementary stateprobe diagnostics measure elite decode error, optimism, and
a shuffled-residual baseline. Their definitions and results are in
Appendix~\ref{app:selection}.

To test adaptive refitting, we fork CEM from candidate-for-candidate identical
first populations. One branch refits on $\hat c$ and the other on $c^*$.
Subsequent samples use the same base Gaussian noise transformed by each
branch's fitted mean and variance. Both branches are evaluated at the same
sequence of simulator snapshots, making the refitting cost the only
intervention.

We also audit whether final CEM populations contain an exactly successful
candidate. This supporting availability analysis is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:availability}.

\subsection{Evaluation splits and uncertainty}

All reported MetaWorld evaluation splits are disjoint from the probe-training
seeds. Population-level intervals are bootstrap confidence intervals clustered
by environment seed; episode rates use Wilson intervals. Uncertainty is over
environment seeds, not checkpoint-training seeds. Exact seed ranges,
resampling counts, and per-cell testing details are in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}.

\subsection{OGBench-Cube audit}

The OGBench extension uses a fixed manifest of 32 snapshots and the 300 final
CEM candidates persisted for each snapshot. We compare four selectors on each
population:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the released predicted-endpoint squared latent-$L_2$ cost;
  \item squared latent $L_2$ after executing each candidate and encoding its
  true rendered endpoint, using the dataset goal image;
  \item the same true-endpoint cost using a goal rendered from the exact future
  state in the same renderer; and
  \item the physically best candidate by cube-to-goal distance.
\end{enumerate}
The same-renderer true-endpoint arm is primary: it removes learned endpoint
prediction and renderer mismatch while retaining the frozen encoder and
terminal squared-$L_2$ rule. Physical regret is selected cube distance minus
the best distance among the same 300 candidates. This isolates endpoint
scoring on fixed populations; it does not rerun search. Full integrity checks
and the evaluation protocol are in Appendix~\ref{app:ogbench}.

\paragraph{MetaWorld implementation integrity.}
After matching renderer construction, image orientation, and goal-frame
selection to the released stack, our Reach control is consistent with its
reported performance ($37.5\%$ versus $44.8\pm8.9\%$)
\citep{jepawms2025}. Appendix~\ref{app:audit} gives the complete protocol.

\paragraph{The failure is task-dependent.}\label{sec:breadth}

The physical cost solves all 16 episodes of button-press, drawer-close, and
window-close. With the same exact outcomes and search budget, latent $L_2$
solves none of button-press or window-close but all of drawer-close
(Table~\ref{tab:breadth}). Door-open and assembly are inconclusive because the
physical control itself solves only $2/16$ and $0/16$; peg-insert-side failed
the pre-specified expert-goal gate. The drawer result shows that latent distance
can work when its visual ordering aligns with the task. For all breadth tasks,
the state reference uses the generic goal-observable state slots specified in
Appendix~\ref{app:audit}.

\subsection{Cost ordering deteriorates on search-induced candidates}
\label{sec:l2audit}

On fresh populations, it retains weak ordering on push ($\rho=0.25,0.27$) but
essentially none on pick-place ($0.02,-0.02$). Its top-10\% recall follows the
same split, and its initial shaped-reference regret is $2.98$--$3.96$\,cm. After
five refits, rank correlation is negative in all four cells and recall falls to
$0.01$--$0.02$. Thus pick-place fails before refitting; on push, weak initial
ordering becomes anti-aligned as CEM updates its population.

\paragraph{Stateprobe as a secondary diagnostic.}
The stateprobe illustrates a distinct warning about average validation. On
held-out expert trajectories, its scalar cost has broad rank agreement
($\rho=0.80$--$0.83$), but reference top-10\% recall is only $0.18$--$0.27$.
On CEM-generated populations, rank agreement falls from $0.43$--$0.55$ to
$0.11$--$0.16$ and elite recall from $0.19$--$0.26$ to $0.07$--$0.10$.
Stateprobe refitting also worsens later candidates by $0.84$--$1.55$\,cm
(Table~\ref{tab:branch}). Thus global held-out rank agreement does not certify
the decision-relevant tail, and search can further degrade it. Full readout,
selection, and shuffled-residual diagnostics are in
Appendix~\ref{app:probe} and~\ref{app:selection}.

Final proxy-guided populations also contain exactly successful candidates only
rarely; this supporting availability audit is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:availability}. It is consistent with earlier refits degrading
proposal quality, but does not separately identify all sources of scarcity.

\subsection{A second encoder shows residual misranking}
\label{sec:ogbench}

On the fixed OGBench populations, replacing the predicted endpoint with its
rendered outcome reduces mean physical regret by $4.51$\,cm [1.84, 7.52] and
raises success from $16/32$ to $21/32$ (Table~\ref{tab:ogbench}). Dynamics error
therefore matters. It is not the whole explanation: true-endpoint latent $L_2$
still leaves $3.29$\,cm [1.49, 5.67] regret and misses four successes found by
the physical selector. Rank agreement improves from $\rho=0.006$ to $0.395$,
but remains imperfect. Using the dataset goal or a goal rendered in the same
world changes mean regret by only $0.03$\,cm.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments reveal two ways a cost can fail. Latent $L_2$ carries little
task-relevant ordering on fresh pick-place candidates and only weak ordering on
push. Stateprobe begins with better ordering, despite being imperfect, but CEM
moves into regions where that ordering degrades. In both cases, updating CEM
with the learned cost produces worse later candidates than updating it with the
physical cost. The OGBench result adds an important qualification: dynamics
error can be a large part of total regret even when cost misranking remains
after that error is removed.

The practical implication is simple. A world model should not be evaluated
only by average prediction error, and a planning representation should not be
evaluated only on held-out trajectories. When simulator access permits, execute
the planner's candidates, score the same outcomes with both learned and
physical costs, and change only the cost used to update search. These tests
indicate whether effort should go
toward the predictor, the terminal objective, or candidate generation.

Our experiments cover two MetaWorld contact tasks with one DINOv2
representation, a cost-only refitting intervention run on eight seeds per
stateprobe cell and sixteen seeds per latent-$L_2$ cell, and one OGBench-Cube
task with an independently trained encoder.
Simulator-state costs and probe labels are evaluation tools, not deployable
inputs. Representation training seeds, alternative probe architectures, and
physical robots remain untested. Non-CEM optimizers are tested only as
planner-level robustness controls; candidate-level ranking and matched-refit
analyses remain CEM-specific. The shuffled-score result separates generic
error magnitude from error structure for the shaped planning cost but not for
direct task distance; that separation for task distance is left open.

\section{Conclusion}

A controlled audit that holds the encoder, terminal cost, and search budget
fixed while replacing learned prediction with exact simulator outcomes
reveals a failure that survives perfect dynamics: in MetaWorld, the same
finite search succeeds with a physical cost while latent $L_2$ and a
supervised state-space cost remain near failure. The reason is not just
inaccurate outcome prediction -- the costs misrank exact outcomes, and CEM
acts on that ranking when it generates later candidates. OGBench-Cube shows
the same residual scoring problem with a distinct, independently trained
encoder, while also confirming that dynamics error can explain a substantial
share of total regret. The drawer-close success shows that the failure is
task-dependent rather than universal. The broader lesson is to test the
ordering a cost induces on the candidates created by search, rather than
infer planning quality from average held-out accuracy.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}
This work studies failure modes of simulation-based robot world-model planning
and may support more reliable evaluation of learned planning systems. The
experiments do not establish real-world robot safety or generality beyond the
evaluated tasks, checkpoints, costs, and planners. Overgeneralizing the
findings could create misplaced confidence in an audit or unwarranted
conclusions about representation learning more broadly. Independent simulator
and physical-system validation, together with deployment-specific safety
controls, remains necessary before applying these conclusions to robots.

\paragraph{Candidate execution.}
For every candidate action sequence, the harness restores an identical MuJoCo
snapshot, executes the sequence, renders the endpoint, and runs the released
encoder on that rendered observation. Simulator state is used only for
evaluation, stateprobe supervision, and the physical reference branch; it is
never supplied to the learned predictor.

\paragraph{Immediate selection.}
The first-population audit evaluates 100 sequences per seed before any CEM
refit. The top-10\% elite sets are independently formed under stateprobe and
reference cost. The shuffled-residual baseline performs 1,000 within-population
permutations. Seed-clustered percentile intervals use 5,000 bootstrap
resamples.

\paragraph{Held-out scalar cost.}
We reconstruct the probes' immutable trajectory split (90/10, seed 0) without
loading or changing the world model. For each non-Reach held-out expert
trajectory, every frame-skip-5 endpoint is scored against that trajectory's
final latent goal using the deployed stateprobe composition and against the
final simulator-state goal using $c^*$. Metrics first reduce within trajectory;
the primary 95\% intervals resample the 11 push/pick-place trajectories; the
all-manipulation aggregate uses all 67 non-Reach trajectories. This test uses
one probe-training seed and expert trajectories; the CEM population test
measures the distribution encountered during planning.

\paragraph{Adaptive branch.}
Both CEM branches share the simulator snapshot, first candidate population,
and base Gaussian noise. Each branch transforms later common noise by its own
mean and variance. The physical-cost branch determines the common sequence of
MPC snapshots, so the comparison isolates search updates at those snapshots.

\paragraph{Candidate availability.}
Candidate-horizon success is evaluated with the simulator's exact task
predicate. It need not imply episode-end success after subsequent MPC replans.
Intervals cluster replans by environment seed. The final populations reflect
all earlier proxy-guided updates.

\section{OGBench-Cube Protocol and Integrity}\label{app:ogbench}

The OGBench audit uses the released
\path{quentinll/lewm-cube} checkpoint and the official
\path{ogbench/cube_single_expert.h5} dataset, with goal offset 25, horizon 5,
action block 5, and 300 persisted final candidates for each of 32 snapshots.
The manifest fixes one snapshot per distinct episode. A candidate is successful
when its final cube-to-goal distance is below $4$\,cm. Snapshot is the unit of
uncertainty, and all reported intervals use 10,000 paired bootstrap draws.

The evaluator creates two independent \texttt{World}/\texttt{MjModel}
instances. Each is initialized once with visual variation disabled, after which
camera, light, material, texture, and geometry signatures must agree. Before
each state render or candidate rollout, it calls \texttt{mj\_resetData}, restores
dataset position and velocity plus the future cube target, calls
\texttt{mj\_forward}, aligns finite-difference previous-state fields, and
disables solver warm-start. The two worlds must agree within $10^{-5}$\,m on
physical distance and exactly on success, executed steps, endpoint pixels, and
selected index; encoder costs must agree within $10^{-5}$. All 32 shards pass,
with zero worst-case repeat error on the reported physical, pixel, latent-cost,
and selection fields.

For an exact initial or goal state, the encoder-domain ratio is the larger
same-state squared-latent discrepancy between evaluator and dataset render,
divided by the smaller initial-to-goal separation in the two render domains.
The precommitted maximum is 0.25; the worst observed ratio is
$6.59\times10^{-4}$. The dataset-goal and same-renderer-goal controls select the
same number of successes and differ by $0.0315$\,cm in mean regret. These checks
establish reproducible, encoder-near-domain endpoint evaluation.

\section{Planner-Level Robustness Control}\label{app:planner}

On DINO-WM push under oracle dynamics, stateprobe remains near failure with CEM
($1/16$), MPPI ($0/16$), and random shooting ($2/16$); latent $L_2$ remains near
failure with MPPI ($0/16$) and random shooting ($0/16$). Their free-space Reach
controls are $16/16$, $13/16$, and $6/16$, respectively. We additionally run a
privileged physical-reference cost on push itself, at the same seeds and
budget, under every tested optimizer: it solves $16/16$ under CEM, $16/16$
under MPPI, and $11/16$ under random shooting. Because this physical reference
converts the same finite search into success under all three optimizers --
including a majority of shooting's non-adaptive, fixed-proposal search -- the
near-failure of stateprobe and latent $L_2$ on push is not explained by
planner capacity alone. The candidate-level and refitting analyses in the
main paper use CEM.
