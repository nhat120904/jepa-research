\title{\textbf{Latent World Models for Robot Planning:}\\[2pt]
\textbf{Representations, Planners, and Failure Modes}\\[6pt]
\large Research Proposal}

\author{VAR Meta}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Planning for robots directly from visual observation has converged on a common
architecture: an encoder maps images to a latent state, an action-conditioned
predictor rolls that state forward, and an optimiser searches over action
sequences for the one whose predicted terminal state minimises a distance to a
goal. The four components of this architecture are studied largely in
isolation: representation objectives in the self-supervised learning
literature, trajectory optimisers in the control literature, end-to-end
success rates in the robotics literature. Because any one of the four can fail
silently, a reported failure is usually reported without a cause.

We propose a research program built around making that attribution tractable.
Its instrument is a taxonomy of four failure surfaces---prediction error,
proposal coverage, transition realizability, and cost geometry under
selection---each carrying an explicit condition that would refute attributing
a failure to it. Read against the literature on representation objectives and
search algorithms, the taxonomy exposes a methodological gap: nearly every
existing repair for the latter three surfaces is evaluated under learned
dynamics, so its reported gain cannot be separated from ordinary prediction
error. The discriminating experiment---rerunning each repair with candidate
dynamics held exact---has, to our knowledge, not been reported for any of
them.

We report a controlled pilot that runs this experiment for one surface.
Executing every candidate in the simulator, a privileged state-based cost
solves a contact-rich task in $64/64$ episodes where the standard latent cost
solves $0/64$ under identical search. A series of repairs aimed at
representation fidelity, run in the same harness, leaves planning unchanged.
That result motivates the plan we propose: costs trained on the distribution
the optimiser itself induces, rather than one fixed by the experimenter; a
decision-equivalence criterion for how much a representation must preserve;
discriminating reruns of the field's existing repairs under exact dynamics;
and an evaluation protocol that reports three quantities current practice
omits---candidate availability, selection regret, and proxy--physical rank
correlation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===============================================================

A world model, in the sense used throughout this proposal, is a learned function
that predicts the consequences of actions well enough to select among them. The
idea is old, but its modern visual form dates to the observation that a compact
recurrent latent state suffices to train a controller entirely inside the
model's own imagination~\citep{ha2018worldmodels}, and to the demonstration that
such a latent can be planned in directly from pixels~\citep{hafner2019planet}.
The subsequent years have produced a large and fast-moving literature built on a
broadly shared architecture: encode, predict, score, search.

That convergence has an awkward consequence. The architecture is a composition
of four blocks, and success requires all four to work, but failure of any one
produces the same observable---a task that does not get solved. The encoder may
discard the information the task needs. The predictor may produce the wrong
future. The search may never propose a good action sequence. The cost may prefer
a physically worse sequence to a better one already present in its own candidate
population. End-to-end success rate, the statistic almost universally reported,
cannot distinguish these cases. Neither can prediction error, which is measured
on a data distribution the planner will not visit.

\paragraph{Scope}
This proposal covers latent world models used for \emph{planning} in robot
manipulation and navigation: models where control is obtained by optimising over
predicted futures at decision time, or by a controller trained against such a
model. We treat representation learning and trajectory optimisation as jointly
determining behaviour, and we cover the evaluation practices that connect them.
We exclude model-free reinforcement learning, video generation evaluated for
perceptual quality alone, and policy learning in which no explicit forward model
is optimised over.

\paragraph{Research questions}
The taxonomy and pilot study below motivate three questions this proposal is
organised to answer.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{Which failure surface binds where?} The diagnostic literature
reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-diag} disagrees on this, but the disagreement
may be an artifact of protocol---different systems, tasks and dynamics
conditions---rather than a real disagreement about mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:lit-synth}). Section~\ref{sec:open} proposes the
discriminating experiment, holding candidate dynamics exact, that would settle
this for the field's existing S3 and S4 repairs.
\item \emph{Can a cost or representation be trained on the distribution the
planner itself induces, rather than one fixed in advance?} Every objective
surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-repr} is fit on a distribution chosen by the
experimenter; what a planner consumes is an ordering on a distribution its own
search produces after refitting (Equation~\eqref{eq:plan}). Whether an objective
can be defined directly on that induced distribution is open, and is the
question Section~\ref{sec:open} treats as most consequential.
\item \emph{Does this failure mode generalise beyond one optimiser and one
representation family?} The pilot evidence in Section~\ref{sec:case} comes from
CEM and two frozen-encoder checkpoints. Section~\ref{sec:open} states what would
need to hold for the mechanism to be a property of sampling-based search against
underdetermined latent costs generally, rather than of this instance.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Approach and preliminary evidence}
Answering these questions needs an instrument, and a demonstration that the
instrument matters. Both are already in hand. A \emph{design-space organisation}
(Section~\ref{sec:lit}) separates what shapes the latent from what searches over
it, making visible that these two choices are usually made independently and
rarely co-designed. A \emph{failure-surface taxonomy}
(Section~\ref{sec:failures}) turns question (1) into an experiment: each surface
carries an explicit refutation condition, and applying it to the literature
shows that the great majority of proposed repairs are evaluated under learned
dynamics, confounding the surface they claim to fix with ordinary prediction
error. A \emph{controlled pilot} (Section~\ref{sec:case}) runs the
discriminating experiment for one surface and finds that removing prediction
error does not, by itself, close the gap---evidence that motivates the specific
directions proposed in Section~\ref{sec:open} rather than settling question (1)
in general.

\paragraph{Organisation}
Section~\ref{sec:background} gives background on world models in robot
learning---what they are, why the field wants them, how they are used, and why
the visual and contact-rich case is hard. Section~\ref{sec:lit} is the
literature review. Section~\ref{sec:failures} develops the failure taxonomy,
Section~\ref{sec:case} reports the case study, and Section~\ref{sec:open} states
the proposed directions.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{What a world model is, and why robot learning wants one}

A world model is an internal, learned model of how an environment responds to
action: given a representation of the current situation and a candidate
action, it predicts what the situation will become. The idea long predates
deep learning---it is the model in model-based control and in Sutton's Dyna
architecture---but unlike a policy, which encodes what to do, a world model
encodes what would happen, so a single model can be reused for any objective
expressible over its predictions rather than being tied to one reward.

Four arguments recur for why robot learning wants this. \emph{Data
efficiency}: robot interaction is expensive in a way simulated benchmarks are
not, and a world model reuses each observed transition many times instead of
consuming fresh environment steps~\citep{ha2018worldmodels,hafner2019planet}.
\emph{Task generality}: scene dynamics are shared across tasks while rewards
are not, so goal-conditioned formulations can serve a new goal at deployment
by supplying a new goal image, with no retraining. \emph{Learning without
reward labels}: most robot data is demonstrations or unstructured
interaction with no reward annotation, which planning can exploit where
model-free methods cannot~\citep{sobal2025pldm}. \emph{Deliberation before
acting}: predicting consequences lets a robot reject a candidate action
before executing it, which matters for safety and for behaviour---recovering
from a novel configuration, composing an undemonstrated sequence---that a
reactive policy obtains only through extensive training.

\subsection{How a world model is used}

Three usages appear in robot learning, and the distinction determines what a
model must be good at. \emph{As a simulator for policy learning}, in the Dyna
tradition matured by Dreamer~\citep{hafner2019planet,hafner2025dreamerv3}, the
model must be accurate on the distribution the improving policy visits, and
its errors are absorbed by that policy's own training. \emph{As a substrate
for planning at decision time}, the usage this proposal is concerned with, the
model is queried inside an optimisation loop at every control step and is
exposed directly to whatever the optimiser chooses to evaluate, including
regions no policy would visit. \emph{As an auxiliary objective}, predicting the
future is used as a self-supervised signal to shape a representation, with
control obtained by other
means. Much recent representation-learning work sits here, and its outputs are
frequently adopted by the planning work reviewed later.

\subsection{The visual problem}

Classical model-based control assumes a compact physical state---positions,
velocities, contact forces---identified offline or derived from known physics. It
works well where that assumption holds and fails where modern robotics wants to
operate. Two obstacles are decisive.

First, \emph{state estimation}. When the only sensor is a camera, the physical
state that classical control needs is not directly available, and hand-designed
perception pipelines that extract it are brittle to clutter, lighting, and
unfamiliar objects.

Second, \emph{model fidelity}. Contact-rich dynamics are stiff and
discontinuous, and depend on physical parameters---friction, mass distribution,
compliance---that are impractical to identify for every object a robot will
encounter.

Learned visual world models address both by declining to estimate physical state
at all. Rather than mapping images to positions and velocities and then
simulating physics, they learn an arbitrary internal representation and learn how
it evolves under action. \citet{ha2018worldmodels} showed that a controller could
be evolved entirely within such a model. \citet{hafner2019planet} made the
learned representation directly plannable, solving pixel-based continuous control
with a sampling optimiser over imagined rollouts, and
DreamerV3~\citep{hafner2025dreamerv3} showed that one configuration of the recipe
transfers across domains without per-task tuning.

The cost of this move is that nothing constrains what the learned state
contains. That freedom is the source of both the approach's generality and the
difficulties surveyed in this proposal.

\subsection{Pixel-space and latent-space prediction}

Visual world models divide by \emph{what} they predict. One family predicts
future observations directly, in pixel space, and has benefited from rapid
progress in video generation. The other predicts a learned representation of the
future and never reconstructs an image.

Two arguments favour latent prediction for control. The first is capacity
allocation: a pixel-space objective spends model capacity in proportion to visual
variance, and in a robot scene the background, lighting and texture dominate that
budget while the task-determining variable often occupies a small fraction of the
frame. The second is the unpredictability of detail: much of what a camera
records cannot be predicted from the current frame and action, and a model forced
to predict it must hedge, producing blurred futures that are poor inputs to a
planner. Predicting a representation permits the model to discard what it cannot
predict, which is the founding argument for joint-embedding
architectures~\citep{lecun2022path}.

Both arguments concern what a representation should \emph{drop}. Neither
constrains the geometry of what survives, and Section~\ref{sec:failures} locates
the field's current difficulty in exactly that gap.

\subsection{Planning with a latent world model}

\paragraph{The model}
Let $o_t$ be an observation and $a_t$ an action. A latent world model comprises
an encoder $z_t = E(o_t)$ and an action-conditioned predictor
$\hat z_{t+1} = f(z_t, a_t)$, applied recursively to obtain an $H$-step rollout
$f^{H}(z_t, a_{1:H})$. Systems differ in what supervises $E$ and $f$: pixel
reconstruction, latent self-prediction, a downstream value, or nothing at all
when $E$ is a frozen pretrained network.

\paragraph{Planning}
Given a goal, planning selects
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plan}
  a_{1:H}^{\star} \;=\; \arg\min_{a_{1:H} \in \mathcal{A}}
  \; c\!\left(f^{H}(z_t, a_{1:H}),\, z_g\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of candidates the optimiser actually
materialises and $c$ is a terminal cost. In goal-image formulations $z_g =
E(o_g)$ and $c$ is Euclidean distance; in reward-based formulations $c$ is the
negative of a learned value. Equation~\eqref{eq:plan} is executed in receding
horizon: a prefix of $a^{\star}_{1:H}$ is applied, the state re-encoded, and the
optimisation repeated.

\paragraph{Two properties of \eqref{eq:plan}}
First, the cost is evaluated on $\mathcal{A}$, a set the optimiser
\emph{chooses}, not on the data distribution on which $E$, $f$ or $c$ were
fitted. Second, what the planner consumes is an \emph{ordering} over
$\mathcal{A}$; the numerical accuracy of $c$ matters only insofar as it induces
the correct ranking. Both properties are routinely elided when models are
evaluated by average prediction or decoding error, and both become central to
the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:failures}.

\subsection{Why this is hard}

Three difficulties are intrinsic to the setting and recur throughout the
literature reviewed next.

\emph{Compounding error.} Predictions are applied recursively, so a small
per-step error grows over a horizon. This bounds how far ahead any learned model
can usefully plan, and it is the difficulty the field understands best.

\emph{Partial observability.} A single camera frame does not determine the
state. Mass, friction, and occluded geometry all affect dynamics and none is
visible, so a model must either carry history or accept that its predictions are
distributions rather than points.

\emph{Contact.} Manipulation dynamics are smooth almost everywhere and
discontinuous exactly where the task is decided. Near contact, a small difference
in action produces a large and qualitatively different outcome, while the visual
difference between the two situations immediately beforehand is slight. This is
the regime in which reported performance is weakest across the systems reviewed
below.

\subsection{Representation objectives}\label{sec:lit-repr}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The representation determines what the planner can express and what it can
distinguish. We group approaches by the objective that shapes the latent,
because that objective---not the architecture---determines what information the
latent is permitted to discard.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction-based latent dynamics}

The founding line trains a latent to support pixel reconstruction and learns
dynamics in that latent. \citet{ha2018worldmodels} compress frames with a
variational autoencoder, learn recurrent dynamics in the code space, and evolve
a small controller inside the model; the striking result was that a policy
trained purely on imagined rollouts transferred back to the environment.
\citet{hafner2019planet} make the latent directly plannable with a recurrent
state-space model combining a deterministic path, which carries information
reliably over time, with a stochastic path that models uncertainty; planning
uses the cross-entropy method on imagined trajectories, and the system solves
continuous control from pixels with far fewer environment steps than model-free
alternatives. DreamerV3~\citep{hafner2025dreamerv3} extends the line with
normalisation and robustness techniques that let one hyperparameter
configuration span domains from continuous control to discrete exploration.

\emph{Assessment.} The reconstruction objective has a well-known liability for
control: it allocates capacity in proportion to pixel variance rather than task
relevance. In the manipulation setting of Section~\ref{sec:background} this is
precisely backwards. The Dreamer line partly escapes the criticism because
reward prediction shapes the latent alongside reconstruction, which is the same
mechanism that Section~\ref{sec:lit-repr-value} identifies as decisive; the
purely reconstructive variants do not.

\subsubsection{Joint-embedding predictive architectures}

Joint-embedding methods predict the \emph{representation} of a target view
rather than reconstructing it, discarding unpredictable detail by
construction~\citep{lecun2022path}. I-JEPA~\citep{assran2023ijepa} instantiates
this for images: a context block predicts the representations of masked target
blocks produced by an exponential-moving-average target encoder, with no
decoder anywhere in the system. Because the objective admits a trivial constant
solution---predict the same vector everywhere---the design of the anti-collapse
mechanism is the defining engineering concern of the family.
VICReg~\citep{bardes2022vicreg} makes the constraints explicit as a sum of
variance, invariance and covariance terms, and LeJEPA~\citep{lejepa2025} pursues
a formulation with fewer heuristics.

V-JEPA 2~\citep{assran2025vjepa2} scales the approach to video and post-trains an
action-conditioned predictor on robot interaction data, planning zero-shot with a
sampling optimiser against latent distance; it remains the principal
demonstration that this family can drive a physical robot. V-JEPA
2.1~\citep{murlabadia2026vjepa21} reports that a dense predictive loss and deeper
self-supervision substantially improve contact-rich grasping over that baseline.
That result matters beyond its own contribution: it is direct evidence that
spatial feature density, and not only the planning objective, governs contact
performance, and Section~\ref{sec:open} treats it as the leading competing
explanation for the findings in Section~\ref{sec:case}.
LeWorldModel~\citep{maes2026leworldmodel} trains encoder and predictor jointly
from pixels, dispensing with the frozen-encoder assumption that most planning
work inherits.

\emph{Assessment.} Two structural facts about this family recur throughout the
rest of this review. There is no decoder, so a latent distance cannot be
inspected to determine what it measures---any claim about what the cost is doing
requires building and separately validating a probe. And the target encoder
observes the true future, which admits solutions in which the predictor matches
its target without making full use of the action input; this is taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:lit-diag-action}.

\subsubsection{Frozen pretrained visual features}

A third strategy avoids representation learning for control altogether: freeze a
strong self-supervised encoder and train only the dynamics.
DINO-WM~\citep{zhou2024dinowm} freezes DINOv2~\citep{oquab2024dinov2} patch
tokens, trains an action-conditioned predictor over them, and plans zero-shot
against latent $L_2$ distance to a goal image, with goals specified visually
rather than as states or rewards. The appeal is modularity and sample
efficiency: one visual backbone serves every task, only the dynamics must be
relearned, and no reward engineering is required.

\citet{jepawms2025} provide the most comprehensive controlled comparison
available for this family, systematically ablating design choices---encoder
frozen versus trained, action-conditioning mechanism, rollout length, cost
form---and releasing checkpoints and a planning stack across several benchmarks.
Their finding that Euclidean latent distance with sampling-based search is the
effective default is why $L_2$ is the object of study in most subsequent
diagnostic work rather than an arbitrary choice. \citet{sobal2025pldm} extend
latent-dynamics planning to reward-free offline data, arguing that planning is
the right way to exploit data with no reward labels, and \citet{nwm2024} apply
the formulation to navigation at scale.

\emph{Assessment.} The unexamined assumption is that a representation optimised
for generic visual prediction induces a geometry in which distance is a usable
planning cost. The pretraining objective never saw a planner, a goal, or an
action. Section~\ref{sec:lit-diag-cost} reviews the accumulating evidence that
this assumption fails, and it fails hardest in exactly the contact regime
described in Section~\ref{sec:background}.

\subsubsection{Value- and task-aware latents}\label{sec:lit-repr-value}

TD-MPC2~\citep{hansen2024tdmpc2} occupies the opposite end of this axis. The
latent is trained jointly with reward and value prediction and is never required
to reconstruct anything; planning uses MPPI with a learned terminal value that
substitutes for the tail of the horizon. The system scales to a single agent
spanning many tasks and embodiments. The line traces back to
MuZero~\citep{schrittwieser2020muzero}, which trains a latent purely to support
its own reward, value and policy predictions---never grounding it in
observations or an explicit dynamics-reconstruction loss at all---and searches
over it with Monte Carlo tree search rather than the sampling-based MPC that
dominates Table~\ref{tab:planners}; TD-MPC2 keeps the value-trained latent but
swaps tree search for MPPI, which is the more practical choice in continuous
action spaces.

\emph{Assessment.} This is the clearest existing demonstration that cost
geometry is a property one can \emph{train for} rather than an inevitable
consequence of latent prediction, and it is the natural comparison for any claim
that planning failure is intrinsic to this class of models. Its limitation is
the requirement for a reward signal, which is precisely what goal-image
formulations are designed to avoid; whether an equivalent can be obtained
reward-free is one of the central open questions of Section~\ref{sec:open}.

\subsubsection{Explicitly structured latents}

A recent group imposes structure that the generic objectives do not.
\citet{kim2026latentstate} frames the problem as latent state design under
sufficiency constraints, organising methods by what their state is \emph{for}
rather than by architecture, and arguing that different downstream
uses---prediction, control, memory, counterfactual reasoning---impose different
and sometimes incompatible sufficiency requirements.
\citet{straightening2026} regularise the curvature of latent trajectories,
motivated by the perceptual straightening hypothesis, so that Euclidean latent
distance better approximates geodesic distance; they report improved
conditioning of the planning objective and higher success on goal-reaching
tasks, which is a direct intervention on the geometry rather than on the cost.
Object-centric and invariance-based variants~\citep{causaljepa2026,
invariantjepwm2026} constrain the latent toward factors the task depends on.

\emph{Assessment.} This group is the most directly responsive to the diagnosis
in Section~\ref{sec:failures}, because it targets the representation--planner
interface explicitly rather than treating representation quality as a proxy for
it. Its evidence base is currently thin and largely on simpler benchmarks.

\subsection{Planning and control algorithms}\label{sec:lit-planners}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{Sampling-based model-predictive control}

The dominant choice is the cross-entropy method~\citep{rubinstein1997cem}:
sample a population of action sequences from a parametric distribution, evaluate
each under the model, retain an elite fraction, refit the distribution to those
elites, and iterate. It requires no gradients, tolerates discontinuous costs,
and parallelises trivially, which is why it appears in nearly every system
reviewed above. iCEM~\citep{pinneri2020icem} adds the refinements that make it
practical in real time---colored-noise sampling that produces temporally
correlated action sequences rather than white noise, momentum on the
distribution parameters, and warm-starting from the previous solution---reporting
order-of-magnitude reductions in the sample budget needed for real-time control.
MPPI~\citep{williams2017mppi} replaces elite truncation with an exponential
weighting derived from a path-integral formulation of stochastic optimal
control.

\emph{Assessment.} The refitting loop deserves emphasis, since it is the
mechanism behind several results in Section~\ref{sec:lit-diag-cost}. These
optimisers do not sample independently: they concentrate the candidate
distribution wherever the cost reads low. If the region where the cost reads low
is a region where the cost is \emph{wrong}, the optimiser moves its own
evaluation set into that region and keeps it there. The distribution on which
the cost is queried is therefore determined jointly by the cost's errors and by
the search, and is not the distribution on which any component was validated.

\subsubsection{Gradient-based planning}

Because $f$ and $c$ are differentiable, one can descend Equation~\eqref{eq:plan}
directly in action space. This is far cheaper per iteration than sampling, but
queries the model in a mode it was never trained for---through input gradients
rather than forward prediction---and reliably produces out-of-distribution or
adversarial action sequences. \citet{parthasarathy2025traintest} characterise
this train--test gap explicitly and repair it by synthesising training data that
smooths the induced planning landscape; \citet{straightening2026} attack the same
problem from the representation side by improving the conditioning of the
objective.

\subsubsection{Amortised and search-free control}

Search at decision time is expensive, and \citet{nguyen2026latentgeometry} ask
whether it is necessary. They replace it with a goal-conditioned inverse
dynamics model mapping current latent, goal latent and remaining horizon
directly to an action, matching or exceeding CEM in seven of eight settings at
two orders of magnitude lower per-decision cost. Their argument is that under a
sufficiently regular latent geometry---their study uses a model whose latent is
explicitly regularised for smoothness and uniformity---much of what search
recovers is already locally encoded, so the search is redundant.

\emph{Assessment.} The study also sweeps CEM, MPPI, iCEM and gradient planning,
making it the clearest available caution against drawing conclusions about
latent planning from a single optimiser. Its positive result is conditional on
the regularised latent it studies, so it does not establish that amortisation
helps for arbitrary representations.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical decomposition}

Long-horizon tasks strain a flat optimiser, whose candidate space grows
exponentially in horizon. \citet{zhang2026hwm} plan hierarchically with latent
world models, decomposing a goal into subgoals so that each level faces a
shorter horizon. The relevance to Section~\ref{sec:failures} is indirect but
important: a shorter horizon leaves a cost less room to be exploited, making
hierarchy a plausible mitigation for cost-geometry failures as well as a
scalability mechanism.

\subsection{Diagnostic and failure-analysis work}\label{sec:lit-diag}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

A distinct body of work, most of it from 2025--2026, studies why these systems
fail rather than proposing new ones. It is the most directly relevant literature
to this proposal and the least settled.

\subsubsection{Dynamics error and its exploitation}

\citet{janner2019trust} establish the classical result: a policy optimised
inside a learned model exploits that model's error, and the damage can be bounded
by limiting rollout length according to model confidence.
\citet{lambert2020objective} sharpen the point into the form most relevant here,
showing that the objective used to train a dynamics model is not monotonically
related to control performance---a model that is better by likelihood can control
worse. \citet{bhamidipaty2026imperfect} state the general case for planning
against imperfect models. The offline setting produced the sharpest version of
the same fix: MOPO~\citep{yu2020mopo} and MOReL~\citep{kidambi2020morel}
penalise a candidate by an estimate of the model's own uncertainty at that
candidate, so the optimiser is pushed away from exactly the region it would
otherwise exploit, rather than being stopped early as in the rollout-length
repair. This surface is comparatively well understood, and its remedies are
standard.

\subsubsection{Proposal coverage}

\citet{imwm2026} make a case that is easy to overlook and hard to dismiss.
Replacing the learned predictor with literal environment rollout while holding
encoder, cost, budget and replanning cadence fixed, they find that on their
failing cells the candidate population contains almost no goal-reaching sequence
at all---so no improvement to the cost could have helped. Their remedies are
correspondingly proposal-side: demonstration retrieval to initialise the search,
a start--goal--action compatibility score, and a reliability gate that modulates
how far the planner trusts the model.

\emph{Assessment.} This is the principal competing explanation for any claimed
cost-geometry failure, and distinguishing the two requires measuring coverage
and selection on the same population. Their substitution of environment rollout
for the learned predictor also establishes priority for the oracle-dynamics
construction used in Section~\ref{sec:case}.

\subsubsection{Transition realizability and action grounding}\label{sec:lit-diag-action}

A rollout may end near the goal while passing through transitions that no
sequence of actions could produce. \citet{acid2026} add per-step inverse-dynamics
cycle residuals to the planning cost with an adaptive scale, rejecting
candidates that the terminal cost alone would prefer, and report improvements
across four action-conditioned world models and six manipulation and navigation
tasks. \citet{liu2026wav} decompose verification into state plausibility and
action reachability using a sparse inverse model and cycle consistency, using
the resulting verifier for self-improvement in under-explored action regimes.
\citet{chen2026atm} build probes on real encoded versus model-predicted
transitions and summarise action-semantic preservation in a transfer matrix,
collapsible into a screening score that ranks checkpoints without planner-coupled
evaluation---reporting order-of-magnitude speedups over simulator rollout for
model selection.

\subsubsection{Cost geometry and selection}\label{sec:lit-diag-cost}

The newest strand, and the one on which the literature has converged most
rapidly. \citet{rcaux2026} observe that a candidate can be close in latent space
yet unreachable within a finite horizon---latent proximity does not imply
dynamical proximity---and add a budget-conditioned reachability head trained from
trajectory offsets and temporal hard negatives.

\citet{trm2026} conduct the most direct study available. Holding encoder,
dynamics, sampler, optimiser, budget and evaluation manifest fixed and replacing
only the terminal cost, they report: a raw latent cost that misranks candidates
despite nearly perfect linear decodability of task coordinates from the same
latent; a true task-state cost reaching full success under identical settings; a
solver-stress control in which substantially more search fails to rescue the
wrong objective; same-candidate selection audits that compare cost orderings on
identical candidate sets; and a subspace intervention showing that the
coordinate rowspace carrying most control utility accounts for under one percent
of raw latent error. They also deploy their repaired metric inside full CEM
rather than evaluating it offline.

\emph{Assessment.} These two works establish that the phenomenon is real and not
an artifact of one system. \citet{trm2026} already covers candidate-level
selection auditing, a state-based positive control, and deployment of a
repaired cost under search. What is absent from this strand is a study
conducted with candidate dynamics held exact, which is the gap
Section~\ref{sec:failures} identifies.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Optimisation against imperfect proxies}\label{sec:lit-proxy}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The cost-geometry strand has a precise analogue outside robotics, and importing
it correctly matters for interpreting results.

\citet{smith2006optimizer} prove the foundational result, in a decision-analysis
setting: selecting the extremum over noisy estimates guarantees that the selected
item's true value is worse than its estimate, \emph{even when every estimate is
unbiased}. The consequence for this literature is methodological and, so far,
largely unobserved: a demonstration that a proxy cost misranks candidates
establishes nothing unless it exceeds a matched-noise baseline, because pure
selection over unbiased noise already produces misranking.

\citet{skalse2022defining} formalise when improving a proxy degrades true
performance, showing that the conditions under which a proxy is
``unhackable'' are restrictive enough to be practically unattainable.
\citet{gao2023overopt} measure the relationship empirically at scale, tracking
gold reward against proxy reward as optimisation pressure increases under both
reinforcement learning and best-of-$n$ selection, and documenting the
characteristic rise-then-fall; that curve shape is what licenses the term
\emph{overoptimisation}, and its absence should preclude the term.
\citet{karwowski2024goodhart} give a measurable definition of optimisation
pressure and study early stopping as a mitigation. The same shape has been
documented for inference-time selection in language
models~\citep{inferencetimehacking2025,gamingverifiers2026}, where verifier-guided
search degrades true quality past a threshold of search effort.

\emph{Assessment.} This literature supplies the vocabulary---proxy, reference,
optimisation pressure---and one non-negotiable control, the matched-noise null.
What it lacks, and what the robotics setting uniquely offers, is physical ground
truth for the selected candidate: in language-model work the ``gold'' signal is
itself a learned or human judgement, whereas a simulator can report exactly where
the object ended up.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evaluation methodology and benchmarks}\label{sec:lit-eval}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

MetaWorld~\citep{yu2020metaworld} supplies most contact-rich manipulation
evaluation in this literature. Its value here is a scriptable success criterion
with an explicit tolerance, which permits the physical ground truth
Section~\ref{sec:case} depends on. Navigation and 2D benchmarks recur as sanity
checks, but several are saturated for modern models and carry little
discriminative power. Video-model benchmarks have begun to test physical
adherence and action-following directly~\citep{mirabench2026}, reporting that
visual fidelity is a poor proxy for action fidelity and that scale does not
reliably improve action following. Paired intervention protocols evaluate causal
response by changing one physical variable at a time~\citep{whatifworld2026},
with \citet{xia2023neuralcausal} supplying the underlying formalism for
counterfactual identification. \citet{wmsurvey2026} provides a wider taxonomy of
the robot-learning world-model landscape.

\citet{yu2026wmeval} argue from a position paper that predictive quality should
be connected to downstream decision quality rather than reported alone.
\citet{hiddenfailure2026} identify a protocol confound with direct consequences
for comparability: terminal-at-horizon scoring can be misaligned with prefix
execution under receding-horizon control, so planning horizon, executed prefix,
terminal versus running cost, and replanning cadence all change the conclusion.

\emph{Assessment.} Three quantities are essentially never reported and each is
cheap to compute given an existing planning stack: whether a physically
successful candidate was present in the population at all; the physical regret of
the selected candidate relative to the best available one; and the rank
correlation between proxy and physical outcome on identical candidates, measured
separately at the first and last search iteration. Success definitions also
differ materially across papers---an any-step latch and a strict episode-end flag
can differ by a large factor on identical runs, enough to reverse a comparison
between systems---and \citet{chen2026atm} additionally warn that diagnostic
scores are not comparable across model families without calibration, since each
model defines its own latent scale.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Synthesis}\label{sec:lit-synth}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Three observations close the review.

\emph{Representation and planner are chosen independently.} Almost every system
reviewed pairs a representation objective selected on self-supervised grounds
with an optimiser selected on control grounds, and the interface between them---the
cost---is assumed rather than designed. The exceptions are instructive:
TD-MPC2 trains the latent against the decision, and the structured-latent group
shapes the geometry deliberately. Both report that the interface improves when
it is treated as a design target.

\emph{The diagnostic literature disagrees, and the disagreement is unresolved.}
IMWM concludes proposal coverage; TRM concludes terminal-metric misranking; ACID
concludes transition realizability. These conclusions are drawn on different
systems, different tasks, and different protocols, so they are not directly
comparable, and the field currently has no way to say which failure binds where.

\emph{The statistical controls are largely absent.} The proxy-optimisation
literature has established for two decades that selection over noisy estimates
degrades true value by itself. Almost no work in latent planning reports the
matched-noise null that this implies, which means the quantitative magnitude of
reported misranking effects is difficult to interpret.

Section~\ref{sec:failures} proposes a taxonomy intended to make the second of
these tractable.

\section{A taxonomy of failure surfaces}\label{sec:failures}
% ===============================================================

Reported failures in this literature are attributed inconsistently, and the
attributions are rarely tested. We propose the taxonomy in
Table~\ref{tab:surfaces}, whose distinguishing feature is the third column: for
each surface, the observation that would \emph{refute} attributing a failure to
it. A taxonomy without refutation conditions organises vocabulary; one with them
organises experiments.

Two observations follow, and they are the analytical core of this proposal.

\emph{First, nearly every proposed repair is evaluated under learned dynamics.}
By the refutation conditions of Table~\ref{tab:surfaces}, a gain measured in that
condition cannot be attributed to S2, S3 or S4 rather than to S1: improving any
component correlated with prediction quality will improve outcomes whenever
prediction is the binding constraint. The discriminating experiment---running the
same repair with exact candidate dynamics, where S1 is closed by
construction---has to our knowledge not been reported for any of the S3 or S4
repairs surveyed above. This is a tractable and high-value gap.

\emph{Second, the field's diagnoses appear to conflict but need not.} The
disagreement noted in Section~\ref{sec:lit-synth} dissolves if these are read not
as mutually exclusive claims about latent planning in general, but as claims
about which surface binds in a particular regime. The useful question is
therefore not which diagnosis is correct but which binds where---a question the
taxonomy makes askable and the current evidence base cannot answer, because the
studies share neither a protocol nor a dynamics condition.

\section{A controlled case study in cost attribution}\label{sec:case}
% ===============================================================

To make the preceding argument concrete, we summarise a study designed around the
refutation conditions of Table~\ref{tab:surfaces}. It is included as an instance
of the methodology this proposal advocates rather than as a general result; its
scope is two released checkpoints and two contact-rich manipulation tasks.

\paragraph{Setting}
The tasks are tabletop manipulation with a simulated Franka arm, the setting in
which the contact difficulty of Section~\ref{sec:background} is most acute. Two
properties of it are load-bearing for what follows. Success is declared at
object-to-goal distances of a few centimetres, so a representation that localises
an object to within ten centimetres is useless for the task while appearing
accurate by most reported metrics. And free-space reaching in the same suite is
comparatively forgiving, so success rates pooled across a task suite can conceal
complete failure on the contact subset---which is why the study reports contact
and free-space cells separately.

\paragraph{Design}
The study closes S1 by construction. At every replanning step the simulator state
is snapshotted; each candidate action sequence is executed in the simulator, its
terminal observation rendered, and that observation encoded by the frozen
encoder. Candidate dynamics are therefore exact while the representation and the
terminal scorer remain the deployed ones. Everything else---encoder, optimiser,
budget, horizon, success criterion---is held fixed across arms, so only the cost
varies. Three costs are compared: the released latent $L_2$; a supervised readout
of object and hand position from frozen tokens, composed into the same functional
form as the reference; and a reference computed from privileged simulator state,
which serves as a positive control for the search budget and is not a deployable
method. Evaluation uses the simulator's task flag at the final environment step
on MetaWorld tasks~\citep{yu2020metaworld}, with checkpoints
from~\citet{jepawms2025}.

\paragraph{Result}
Table~\ref{tab:terminal} reports the comparison. Under identical search, the
state-based reference solves push in $64/64$ episodes and pick-place in $49/64$,
while the latent cost used by the released systems solves $0/64$ in every cell.
The supervised readout reaches at most $5/64$ despite held-out object decoding
error of $1.64$--$1.71$\,cm, comfortably inside the $5$\,cm success radius. Since
prediction error is zero by construction and the planner demonstrably suffices,
the binding constraint in this setting is the mapping from representation to
cost.

\paragraph{Mechanism}
Why an apparently accurate readout fails is answered only by conditioning on
the optimiser. Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} shows agreement between the readout
cost and the physical reference on the optimiser's own candidate populations:
moderate on the initial population, collapsed on the population the optimiser
produces after refitting. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} quantifies the
consequence for selection. The excess regret over a residual-matched
permutation null exceeds the optimizer's-curse baseline of
\citet{smith2006optimizer}, with all confidence intervals above zero, and
intervening on the refitting cost alone degrades the best candidate
subsequently available in the same direction in all eight seeds. Exact-success
candidates remain sparse in final populations, which is a partial concession
to the coverage account of~\citet{imwm2026}: availability is measured after
prior refits, so it does not independently establish a proposal failure.

\paragraph{Scope}
Two checkpoints from one design lineage, one simulator, one task family;
uncertainty is over environment seeds, not over checkpoint training seeds. The
study does not establish that latent costs fail in general, nor that scale cannot
help---indeed \citet{murlabadia2026vjepa21} is direct evidence that denser
features improve contact performance, and running a dense-feature checkpoint
through the same protocol is the most informative single extension available.

\paragraph{Tier 1: closing the methodological gap with the existing harness}
The gap identified in Table~\ref{tab:matrix} is directly actionable: rerunning
the S3 and S4 repairs surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-diag}---inverse-consistency
costs, reachability heads, learned terminal metrics---under exact candidate
dynamics would establish, for each, whether its gain survives when prediction
error is removed. Running Table~\ref{tab:terminal}'s protocol on a
dense-feature checkpoint~\citep{murlabadia2026vjepa21} does the same for the
leading alternative to the cost-geometry account of Section~\ref{sec:case}:
denser predictive features and cost geometry make opposite predictions for
this one experiment, so it adjudicates between the two research programmes
rather than merely adding evidence to one. Both experiments produce, as a
byproduct, the three quantities identified in Section~\ref{sec:lit-eval} as
cheap and essentially unreported; standardising their computation is a
contribution available independently of either outcome.

\paragraph{Tier 2: testing generality}
Almost all evidence for cost-geometry failure comes from CEM. Since the
mechanism depends on distribution refitting---Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}'s
feedback arrow---it should transfer to MPPI and iCEM and behave differently
under gradient or amortised control, but that is a prediction, not yet a
finding; \citet{nguyen2026latentgeometry} is the only study in this area
sweeping several optimisers, and for a different purpose. Hierarchical
decomposition~\citep{zhang2026hwm} tests a different kind of generality: it
shortens the horizon each cost must span, leaving less room for a candidate
to reach a low-cost region that is physically wrong, so it is a plausible
mitigation for cost-geometry failure as well as a scalability mechanism.

\paragraph{Tier 3: the new-objective bet}
The recurring pattern of Table~\ref{tab:nulls}---fidelity improves, planning
does not---has a structural reading. Every objective in
Section~\ref{sec:lit-repr} optimises how well a learned quantity matches a
physical one on a distribution chosen by the experimenter; what a planner
consumes is an ordering on a distribution chosen by the \emph{optimiser},
after refitting has moved it. Objectives defined on rankings within realised
populations, rather than on values over a fixed dataset, are largely
unexplored; the limited evidence available suggests scalar regression onto
true cost is not the right form, while group-relative ranking objectives are
more promising and essentially untested at scale. If such an objective shows
partial traction, the natural follow-on is \citet{kim2026latentstate}'s
sufficiency framing applied to planning specifically: the natural criterion is
preservation of the $\arg\min$ over a family of goals, weaker than
reconstructing the state and stronger than predicting the next latent, which
TD-MPC2~\citep{hansen2024tdmpc2} achieves through value supervision but which
no reward-free, goal-image method yet obtains.

% ===============================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===============================================================

Latent world models for robot planning have converged on a common architecture
whose components are studied separately and evaluated jointly, which makes
failures hard to attribute. We gave background on how this architecture displaced
classical system identification, reviewed the literature along the axes of
representation objective and search algorithm, and derived a four-surface
taxonomy in which each attribution carries an explicit refutation condition.
Applying it exposes a methodological gap: most proposed repairs are evaluated
under learned dynamics, where the surface they claim to fix is confounded with
ordinary prediction error. A controlled study removing that confound places the
binding constraint, in the contact-rich setting tested, on the mapping from
representation to cost rather than on prediction---and a series of repairs
targeting fidelity leaves planning unchanged.

The through-line is a mismatch of quantities. Representations in this literature
are trained to be faithful on distributions chosen by the experimenter; planners
consume orderings on distributions chosen by the optimiser. Most of the
directions in Section~\ref{sec:open} are versions of the question of how to close
that gap, and the field currently lacks both the objectives and the evaluation
protocol needed to answer it.
